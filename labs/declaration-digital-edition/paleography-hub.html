1. Physical description

  • Support: Animal parchment (skin), roughly rectangular, original dimensions ≈ 29½ × 24 inches.
  • Ink: Iron-gall ink. Originally near-black; oxidized and faded to warm brown / near-illegible in many areas due to prolonged light exposure, rolling, and handling.
  • Scribe: Timothy Matlack, clerk of the Continental Congress. Large, formal engrossing hand with flourished title.
  • Date of engrossing: Primarily 19 July – 2 August 1776 (most signatures added 2 August).
  • Current housing: Titanium / aluminum / argon-filled encasement in the Rotunda for the Charters of Freedom, National Archives, Washington, D.C.

2. Layout & paleographic features

  • Title block: Centered, monumental capitals + flourished “The unanimous Declaration…”
  • Body: Single continuous column of text filling nearly the full width of the skin.
  • Signatures: Six vertical columns arranged geographically by state (Georgia far left → New Hampshire far right). John Hancock’s signature is the largest and most prominent.
  • Evidence of use: Dirtied edges from handling, slight trimming of original margins, light-induced ink loss across the entire surface.

3. Restoration pipeline (Codex-style)

  1. Source: National Archives public-domain scan of the engrossed parchment.
  2. Faithful contrast recovery preserving parchment texture (MN4Q9).
  3. Clean scholarly black-on-white (VUmKX) and pure inverse black/white (9lFxo).
  4. Artistic illuminated manuscript treatment with gold capitals & borders (2JNeL).
  5. Scholarly annotation layer with callouts (lX2iq).
  6. Earlier clean scholarly intermediate (5YzpQ).

Extreme original fading means restored letter forms prioritize legibility. For absolute textual accuracy consult the official NARA transcript.

4. Historical context for paleographers

  • The engrossed copy is the official signed version, not the printed Dunlap broadside of 4–5 July.
  • Matlack’s hand is a professional 18th-century clerical engrossing style, not a personal “secretary hand.”
  • Iron-gall ink’s acidity helped it “bite” the parchment (aiding longevity of remaining strokes) but also contributed to eventual brittleness and fading under light.
  • Modern imaging (including multispectral work by NARA and partners) continues to recover details invisible to the naked eye.
